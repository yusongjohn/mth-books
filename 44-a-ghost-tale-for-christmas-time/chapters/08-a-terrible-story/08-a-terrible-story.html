8. A Terrible Story 8. 一个可怕的故事

“What do you think is wrong with him?” said Annie. “你觉得他哪里不对劲？”安妮说。

“I don’t know,” said Jack. He put the wallet in his bag. “But so far our mission’s been a disaster.” “我不知道，”杰克说。 他把钱包放进包里。 “不过到目前为止，我们的任务简直是一场灾难。”

“Well, we can’t give up now,” said Annie. “Come on, let’s follow him.” “那我们现在也不能放弃，”安妮说。 “快点，我们跟上他。”

As Jack and Annie started after Charles, the street was quiet and empty. It seemed the blinding fog and damp drizzle had driven everyone inside. 杰克和安妮开始追赶查尔斯时，街道上安静又空无一人。 看起来，刺眼的浓雾和潮湿的小雨把大家都赶进了屋里。

They hadn’t gone far before Charles looked back and saw them. “Don’t follow me, children!” he called. “Please! I want to be alone now! I must be alone.” 他们还没走远，查尔斯就回头看见了他们。 “孩子们，别跟着我！”，他喊道。 “求求你！ 我现在想一个人待着！我必须一个人！”

Jack and Annie watched Charles vanish into the mist. 杰克和安妮看着查尔斯消失在雾中。

“Maybe we should go back,” Jack said. “We could find our way to his house and wait for him there.” “也许我们应该回去，”杰克说。 “我们可以找到他的家，在那里等他。”

“Something tells me he’s not going home soon,” said Annie. “I feel like he really needs our help now.” “我有种感觉，他不会很快就回家，”安妮说。 “我觉得他现在真的很需要我们的帮助。”

Jack sighed. “Yeah, I feel that way, too,” he said. “So let’s follow him. But we’d better keep a safe distance from him, so he doesn’t see us.” 杰克叹了口气。 “是啊，我也这么觉得，”他说。 “那我们跟上他吧。 但我们最好和他保持安全距离，别让他发现我们。”

As Jack and Annie followed Charles through the fog, they could hear the tapping of his walking stick on the cobblestones. They followed the sound down a sloping street lined with shacks. 杰克和安妮跟着查尔斯穿过浓雾时，能听到他的拐杖敲击鹅卵石的声音。 他们跟着声音走下一条两边都是破房子的斜坡街道。

In the dingy light of the gas streetlamps, Jack could see garbage in the gutters—cabbage leaves, moldy bread, rotten fish. The poor neighborhood frightened him. But he and Annie kept going, following the sounds of Charles’s footsteps and the tapping of his walking stick. 在煤气路灯昏暗的灯光下，杰克看到排水沟里有垃圾——卷心菜叶、发霉的面包、腐烂的鱼。 这个贫穷的街区让他感到害怕。 但他和安妮继续往前走，跟着查尔斯的脚步声和拐杖敲击的声音。

Then the sounds stopped. 然后，声音突然停了下来。

“Wait,” Jack whispered. “等等，”杰克小声说。

Jack and Annie froze. They couldn’t see Charles through the fog, but they could hear the sound of sobbing. 杰克和安妮僵在原地。 他们看不见查尔斯，因为浓雾挡住了视线，但他们能听到抽泣的声音。

“Oh, no!” whispered Annie. “哦，天哪！”安妮小声说。

Jack and Annie moved closer to the sound. Charles was sitting under a streetlamp at the bottom of the hill. His head was resting in the crook of his arm. 杰克和安妮朝声音的方向靠近。 查尔斯正坐在山脚下的路灯下。 他的头靠在手臂的弯处。

“Charles?” said Annie. She stepped closer to Charles. “Are you okay?” Annie sat on the curb next to him. “查尔斯？”安妮说。 她走近查尔斯。 “你还好吗？”安妮坐在他旁边的人行道台阶上。

Jack sat on the other side of Charles. “You want to talk about it?” he asked. 杰克坐在查尔斯的另一边。 “你想谈谈这件事吗？”他问。

Charles lifted his head. “I’m sorry. I’ve never told anyone this story before,” he said. “Not even my wife or my closest friends.” 查尔斯抬起了头。 “对不起。 我以前从没跟任何人说过这个故事，”他说。 “就连我的妻子和最亲密的朋友也没说过。”

“You can tell us,” said Jack. “你可以告诉我们，”杰克说。

Charles looked at Jack, then at Annie. He stood and motioned for them to join him. Then he pointed into the fog. “Over there, near the river, was once a shoe polish factory. It was a tumbledown old building, filled with river rats. I went to work there when I was twelve. I sat at a table, pasting labels onto pots of black polish. I worked eleven hours a day, six days a week, yet I barely earned enough to keep alive.” 查尔斯看着杰克，然后看着安妮。 他站起来，示意他们跟他一起过去。 然后他指着雾中说道。 “那边，靠近河边，曾经有一家鞋光亮剂工厂。” 这是一座破旧的老房子，里面住满了河鼠。 我十二岁的时候就在那里工作了。 我坐在桌子前，把标签贴在装着黑色亮漆的罐子上。 我每天工作十一个小时，每周六天，可我 barely 赚到足够的钱来维持生活。

“You were only twelve?” said Jack. “你当时才十二岁？”杰克说。

“Yes. And I lived alone. I had lost everything—my family, my school, my dignity,” said Charles. “是的。 我当时一个人住。 我失去了一切——我的家人、我的学校、我的尊严，”查尔斯说。

“Were you an orphan?” asked Annie. “你是孤儿吗？”安妮问。

“No. I had parents,” said Charles. “不。” “我有父母，”查尔斯说。

“Why did they make you work in such a bad place?” asked Jack. “他们为什么让你在这么糟糕的地方工作？”杰克问。

“My father had fallen on hard times,” said Charles. “He was a good man, but he couldn’t pay his bills. So he was sent to a debtors’ prison across the river. My mother chose to live there with him.” “我父亲遇到了困难，”查尔斯说。 “他是个好人，但他付不起账单。 所以他被送到河对岸的一座债务人监狱里。 我妈妈选择和他一起住在那里。”

“That’s a terrible story,” said Annie. “那是一个可怕的故事，”安妮说。

“Wait a minute,” said Jack. “You mean your dad was sent to prison because he couldn’t pay his bills?” “等一下，”杰克说。 “你是说你爸爸因为没钱付账单被送进监狱了？”

“Yes,” said Charles. “是的，”查尔斯说。

“That doesn’t make sense,” said Jack. “How could he earn the money to pay his bills if he was in prison?” “这不太对劲，”杰克说。 “如果他进监狱了，怎么赚到钱来付账单呢？”

“That’s a very good question,” said Charles. “这是个非常好的问题，”查尔斯说。

“That doesn’t happen anymore,” said Jack, thinking of life back in Frog Creek. “现在不会再发生这样的事了，”杰克说，想着在蛙溪镇的生活。

“Oh, yes, it does,” said Charles. “Life is still miserable for thousands of poor. Parents live in prisons and workhouses, while countless children work in factories and mines for pennies.” “哦，是的，确实如此，”查尔斯说。 “对成千上万贫穷的人来说，生活仍然很悲惨。 父母住在监狱和济贫院里，而数以万计的孩子则在工厂和矿井里为了一便士工作。”

“But at least things are different for you now,” said Annie. “You’re a famous writer. That should make you feel better.” “但至少你现在的情况已经不一样了，”安妮说。 “你是一位著名的作家。” 那应该会让你感觉好一些。”

“How can that make me feel better?” said Charles. “What is writing? Just ink on a page. It’s not food for the hungry. It’s not medicine for the sick. Lately I’ve been thinking I should give up my writing altogether.” “这怎么能让我感觉好一些呢？”查尔斯说。 “什么是写作？ 就是纸上的一点墨水。 它不是饥饿者的食物。 它不是病人的药。 最近我一直在想，我应该彻底放弃写作。”

“Oh, no,” said Jack. “You can’t do that.” “哦，不，”杰克说。 “你不能这么做。”

“It seems so foolish and vain,” said Charles. “这似乎很愚蠢和虚荣，”查尔斯说。

“But—” said Jack. “但是—”杰克说。

“No,” said Charles. He let out a shuddering sigh. “I have decided: I shall write no more.” “不，”查尔斯说。 他发出一声颤抖的叹息。 “我决定了：我再也不写了。”

“Charles, what about—” said Annie. “查尔斯，关于—”安妮说。

“Be kind, children, and leave me now,” said Charles. “I need to be alone. My heart dies inside of me.” “孩子们，要善良，现在离开我吧，”查尔斯说。 “我需要一个人待着。 我的心里充满了悲伤。”

“Oh. Okay …,” said Annie. She and Jack stood up. “Bye, Charles …” “好吧……，”安妮说。 她和杰克站了起来。 “再见，查尔斯……”

Jack started to wish Charles good luck, but the words stuck in his throat. There was really nothing to say. As Charles covered his face in despair, Jack and Annie started back up the hill. 杰克想给查尔斯祝福，但话卡在了喉咙里。 真的没什么好说的。 当查尔斯绝望地捂住脸时，杰克和安妮开始往山上走。

“We can’t just leave him,” said Jack. “我们不能就这样丢下他，”杰克说。

“I know. So let’s stay nearby,” said Annie. “我知道。 那我们就待在附近吧，”安妮说。

Jack and Annie found a stoop to sit on, and they watched over the lonely figure at the bottom of the hill. 杰克和安妮找到了一个台阶坐下，他们看着山脚下那个孤独的身影。

“Now I know why Merlin sent us here,” said Jack. “But this really seems hopeless.” “现在我知道为什么梅林派我们来这里了，”杰克说。 “但这看起来真的毫无希望。”

“Look in our research book,” said Annie. “Maybe it can help us.” “看看我们的研究书，”安妮说。 “也许它能帮上忙。”

Jack pulled out their book and looked up Charles Dickens again. He read aloud: 杰克拿出他们的书，又查了一遍查尔斯·狄更斯。他大声读道：

Charles Dickens was born in England in 1812. He is one of the most famous writers of all time. He wrote many novels, including Oliver Twist, A Christmas Carol, and— 查尔斯·狄更斯于1812年出生在英格兰。 他是有史以来最著名的作家之一。 他写了许多小说，包括《雾都孤儿》、《圣诞奇遇记》和——

“Wait, wait,” said Annie. “ A Christmas Carol? Wasn’t that the name of the play we saw last year with Mom and Dad and Grandma?” “等一下，等一下，”安妮说。 “《圣诞奇遇记》？ 那不是去年和爸爸妈妈还有奶奶一起看的那出戏的名字吗？”

“Yeah,” said Jack, “on Christmas Eve. Oh, man, that’s why the name Charles Dickens sounded familiar.” “对，”杰克说，“那是平安夜上演的。” 哦，天哪，这就是为什么查尔斯·狄更斯这个名字听起来这么熟悉。”

“Look, there’s a picture of Scrooge, the mean guy in the play,” said Annie. She pointed to a picture in the book. It showed an old man wearing a nightcap and holding a candle. “看，这儿有一张斯克鲁奇的照片，就是那出戏里那个坏蛋，”安妮说。 她指着书里的一个图片。 图片上是一个戴夜帽、拿着蜡烛的老人。

Jack read the caption underneath: 杰克读了下面的说明文字：

A Christmas Carol has been retold again and again in plays, movies, and television shows. To this day, it inspires people to be kinder and more generous to others. 《圣诞奇遇记》已经被一遍又一遍地改编成戏剧、电影和电视节目。 直到今天，它仍然激励人们对他人心怀善意、更加慷慨。

“Wow, remember the three ghosts who visit Mr. Scrooge?” said Jack. “The Ghost of Christmas Past, the Ghost of Christmas Present, and the Ghost of Christmas Future.” “哇，还记得那三个去拜访斯克鲁奇先生的鬼魂吗？ 斯克鲁奇？”杰克说。 “过去的圣诞幽灵、现在的圣诞幽灵和未来的圣诞幽灵。”

“Right. They try to change him by showing him his past, his present, and his future,” said Annie. “At the end of the story, he’s like a different person. I can’t believe Charles wrote that story!” “他们想通过让他看到过去、现在和未来来改变他，”安妮说。 “故事结束时，他就像一个完全不同的人了。 我简直不敢相信查尔斯写了这个故事！

“Well, now it seems like he won’t,” said Jack. “He just said he’s never going to write again. He said his heart has died.” “嗯，现在看起来他好像不会写了，”杰克说。 “他刚刚说他再也不想写了。 他说他的心已经死了。

Annie stood up and brushed herself off. “So get out the violin and the bow,” she said matter-of-factly. 安妮站起来，拍了拍身上的灰尘。 “那把小提琴和琴弓拿过来，”她若无其事地说。

“Oh, I forgot all about our magic violin!” said Jack. “哦，我完全忘了我们的魔法小提琴！”，杰克说。

“Me too, until now,” said Annie. “Get it out. I’ll make up a song.” “我也是，直到现在才想起来，”安妮说。 “把它拿出来。” “我会编一首歌。”

“What kind of song?” asked Jack. “什么歌？”杰克问。

“I’ll make up my own version of A Christmas Carol, ” said Annie. “But it won’t be a story about Scrooge. It’ll be a story about Charles—a ghost story about Charles Dickens.” “我会编一首《圣诞颂歌》的改编版。”安妮说。 “但这不是关于斯克鲁奇的故事。” “这是关于查尔斯的——一个关于查尔斯·狄更斯的鬼故事。”
