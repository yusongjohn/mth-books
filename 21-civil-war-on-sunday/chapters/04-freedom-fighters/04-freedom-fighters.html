4. Freedom Fighters 4. 自由战士

Jack carried the water bucket into the tent. 杰克提着水桶走进帐篷。

The scene inside was like a nightmare. 里面的情景像一场噩梦。

The tent was hot and stuffy. A dozen injured soldiers lay on small cots. Some called for food. Others begged for water or just moaned. 帐篷里又热又闷。 十来个受伤的士兵躺在小折叠床上。 有人呼叫要食物。 另一些人恳求要水，或者只是呻吟。

Jack wanted to rush back outside. But Annie got right to work. She rolled up her sleeves and smiled. 杰克想冲回外面去。 可安妮立刻开始干了起来。 她卷起袖子，笑了。

“Hi, everybody!” she said cheerfully. “大家好！”她开心地喊道。

None of the soldiers smiled back. 没有一个士兵笑着回应。

“I have good news!” she said. “We’ve brought lunch!” “我有好消息！”她说。 “我们带来了午餐！”

Annie moved down the row of cots. She handed out pieces of bread and chunks of potatoes to all the patients. 安妮沿着一排行军床走过去。 她把一片片面包和一块块土豆分给所有病人。

“You’ll be feeling better soon,” she said to one sick man. “You’ll see your family again,” she told another. “你很快就会好起来的，”她对一个生病的人说。 “你会再见到家人的，”她对另一个说。

Jack looked around nervously. He wasn’t sure what to do. 杰克紧张地四处张望。 他不知道该做什么。

“Give them water, Jack!” Annie called to him. “给他们水，杰克！”安妮朝他喊道。

Jack saw a tin cup beside each man’s cot. He picked up the first cup. Carefully, he used the ladle to fill it with water. 杰克看见每个男人床边都有一只锡杯。 他拿起第一只杯子。 他小心翼翼地用大汤勺把杯子装满水。

Keeping his eyes down, Jack handed the cup to the patient. He felt shy and uncomfortable. He didn’t know what to say. 杰克低着眼睛，把杯子递给伤员。 他觉得害羞，很不自在。 他不知道该说什么。

Jack moved on to the next patient, then the next. He gave each wounded man a cup of water. But he never looked right at any of them or spoke a word. 杰克走到下一个伤员，再下一个。 他给每个受伤的人一杯水。 可他从来没有正眼看过他们任何人，也没说过一句话。

Soon Jack and Annie had finished passing out food and water. 很快，杰克和安妮就把食物和水分发完了。

“Good-bye!” Annie said. “再见！”安妮说。

She waved and left the tent. Jack quickly followed her. 她挥挥手，离开帐篷。 杰克赶紧跟着她。

“Let’s go home now,” he begged once they were outside. “We’ve got what we came for.” 一到外面，他就恳求道：“我们现在回家吧。” “我们要找的已经找到了。”

“If we leave now, the patients in the next tent will go hungry and thirsty,” said Annie. “要是我们现在走，下一顶帐篷里的病人就会挨饿受渴。”安妮说。

Jack sighed. 杰克叹了口气。

“Okay,” he said. “But after we take care of them, we’re leaving for sure.” “好吧，”他说。 “不过等我们照顾完他们，我们一定走。”

He followed Annie into the next tent. 他跟着安妮进了下一个帐篷。

Just like the last tent, it was filled with wounded soldiers. But the soldiers in this tent were all African-Americans. 和上一个一样，里面满是受伤的士兵。 但这顶帐篷里的士兵全是非裔美国人。

“Hi, everybody!” Annie said, smiling warmly. “大家好！”安妮温暖地笑着说。

Again, she passed out potatoes and bread. She also talked and made jokes. 她又分发土豆和面包。 她还聊天、开玩笑。

Jack poured water into each of the tin cups. Again, he didn’t speak to any of the soldiers. But as he handed over the last cup, a patient spoke to him. 杰克往每只锡杯里倒水。 这一次，他同样没有对任何一个士兵说话。 可当他把最后一杯递过去时，一个伤员对他说话了。

“Thank you for your kindness, son,” the soldier said. “谢谢你的善良，孩子。”那个士兵说。

Jack glanced shyly at the man on the cot. He was an elderly, silver-haired black man. 杰克害羞地瞥了一眼床上的人。 他是一位上了年纪、满头银发的黑人。

“You’re welcome,” said Jack. “不客气，”杰克说。

He tried to think of something else to say. He remembered Annie’s cheerful words. 他想再找点话说。 他想起安妮那些开朗的话。

“You’ll get well soon,” he told the patient. “You’ll be with your family again.” “你很快就会好起来的。”他对那位病人说。 “你会再和家人在一起的。”

The man shook his head. 那人摇了摇头。

“No. I’ll never be with my family again,” he said quietly. “My wife and children were sold long ago.” “不是，那是魔法！一团旋转的光！”安妮说。 我永远不会再和家人在一起了，”他平静地说。 “我的妻子和孩子很久以前就被卖掉了。”

“Sold?” said Jack. “卖了？”杰克说。

“Yes. We were slaves,” the man said. “对。 “我们曾经是奴隶。”那人说。

“You were a slave?” asked Jack. “你以前是奴隶？”杰克问。

“All of us in this tent were once slaves,” the man said. “We ran away from our owners in the South to fight to end slavery, to fight for freedom for our people. I ran barefoot for over thirty miles to tell the Union soldiers that the Confederates were going to attack.” “这个帐篷里的人曾经都是奴隶。”那人说。 “我们从南方的主人那儿逃出来，为结束奴隶制而战，为我们的同胞争取自由。 我赤脚跑了三十多英里，去告诉联邦军士兵，邦联军要来进攻了。”

The man fell silent. 那人沉默下来。

“You’re a very brave freedom fighter,” said Jack. “你是一位非常勇敢的自由战士。”杰克说。

“Thank you, son.” The man closed his eyes. “谢谢你，孩子。”那人闭上眼睛。

Jack wanted to know more about slavery. But he didn’t want to bother the weary patient. He pulled out the Civil War book.

He found a picture of African-Americans standing on a platform. The men, women, and children had chains on their hands and feet.

Jack read:

In the 1800s, the United States was divided over the issue of slavery. The North wanted the country to end all slavery. But the South wanted to keep slaves because more than four million African-American slaves worked in the huge plantation fields there. This disagreement between the North and South led to the Civil War. 在1800年代，美国因为奴隶制的问题而分裂。北方希望国家彻底废除奴隶制。但南方想继续保留奴隶，因为那里有超过四百万的非裔美国奴隶在巨大的种植园里工作。北方和南方之间的这种分歧导致了南北战争。

Jack looked down at the man’s face. He looked very weary.

Jack pulled the nurse’s list of rules from his knapsack.

Lessen sorrow and give hope, he read.

Jack put the list away. He leaned close to the man and spoke in a soft voice: “One day your great-great-grandchildren will be doctors and lawyers,” he said.

The man opened his eyes.

Jack went on. “They’ll help run the government and schools. They’ll be senators and generals and teachers and principals.”

The man stared hard into Jack’s eyes.

“Can you see the future, son?” he asked.

Jack nodded. “In a way … ” he said.

The man smiled a beautiful smile.

“Thank you, son,” he said. Then he closed his eyes again.

“Good luck,” whispered Jack. He hoped the brave man would live to enjoy freedom.

“Ready to go home now, Jack?” said Annie. She had finished passing out the food.

Jack nodded.

As he and Annie stepped out of the tent, they heard someone shout, “She’s back!”

A horse-drawn wagon was barreling into the camp.

“Who’s back?” asked Annie.

“Clara Barton,” a patient said. “She runs this hospital.”

“Clara Barton!” said Annie. “I don’t believe it!”

“Who’s Clara Barton?” asked Jack. He’d heard the name before. But he couldn’t remember who she was.

“Who’s Clara Barton?” said Annie. “Are you nuts?”

She ran to meet the wagon.
