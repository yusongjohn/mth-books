6. The Banana Tree 6. 香蕉树

“This is your castle?” said Jack. “这就是你的城堡？”杰克说。

Basho smiled. “In my heart, my humble cottage is grander than all the castles of the samurai,” he explained. “And my banana tree is more beautiful to me than all the beauty of the Imperial Garden.” 巴肖笑了。 “在我心里，我的小茅屋比所有武士的城堡都要宏伟，”他解释道。 “对我来说，我的香蕉树比皇室花园里的一切都要美丽。”

Jack and Annie stared at the large plant with the long, droopy leaves. 杰克和安妮盯着那棵长着又长又垂下来的叶子的大植物。

“I like this tree so much I have taken my name from it,” said Basho. “ Basho means ‘banana tree.’” “我这么喜欢这棵树，以至于我用它的名字给自己取了名字，”巴肖说。 “巴肖的意思是‘香蕉树’。”

“Cool,” said Annie. She looked around. “It’s nice here.” “真酷，”安妮说。 她环顾四周。 “这里真不错。”

Not really, thought Jack. The cottage was shabby and the droopy banana tree looked scrawny and sad to him. 其实不然，杰克心想。 小屋破旧不堪，那棵垂着叶子的香蕉树在他眼里看起来又瘦又可怜。

“Please come inside,” said Basho. He slipped off his sandals and left them outside. He picked up a bundle of wood, then ducked through the low door that led into his hut. “请进来，”巴肖说。 他脱下拖鞋，放在外面。 他拿起一捆木头，然后弯下腰，从低矮的门走进了他的小屋。

Jack and Annie took off their shoes, too, and followed Basho into a small, shadowy room. 杰克和安妮也脱下鞋子，跟着巴肖走进了一个又小又暗的房间。

Basho opened his shutters to let in the evening air. “Please sit,” he said. 巴肖打开窗户，让傍晚的空气进来。 “请坐，”他说。

“Thank you,” said Jack and Annie. Jack looked around the room for chairs, but there weren’t any. The only furniture was a low wooden table and a bamboo chest. Three straw mats covered the earthen floor. Jack and Annie sat down on one of the mats. “谢谢，”杰克和安妮说。 杰克在房间里四处张望，想找椅子，但没有找到。 房间里唯一的家具是一张低矮的木桌和一个竹制的箱子。 三块草席铺在泥土地板上。 杰克和安妮坐在其中一个垫子上。

Basho lit a small oil lamp. Then he made a fire in his fireplace. “I will prepare tea for us,” he said. “Rest while I draw water from the river.” He picked up one of the two wooden buckets near the door and headed outside. 巴肖点燃了一盏小油灯。 然后他在壁炉里生了一堆火。 “我要为我们准备茶，”他说。 “我休息一下，去河边打水。”他拿起门边两个木桶中的一个，走了出去。

When Basho was gone, Jack and Annie looked at each other. “I guess this is a three-mat house,” said Annie. 当巴肖离开后，杰克和安妮互相看了看。 “我想这是一座三块垫子的房子，”安妮说。

Jack nodded. “You’d think a famous teacher of the samurai would have a hundred-mat house … or at least a fifty-mat house,” he said. 杰克点了点头。 “你可能会以为一位武士的著名老师会有一百块垫子的房子……或者至少有五十块垫子的房子，”他说。

“I like this house, though,” said Annie. “It’s cozy.” “不过，我喜欢这栋房子，”安妮说。 “这里真舒服。”

“I wonder who Basho is exactly,” said Jack. “我想知道巴肖到底是谁，”杰克说。

“If he’s famous, maybe he’s in our book,” said Annie. “Look him up.” “如果他很有名，也许他在我们的书里，”安妮说。 “去查查他。”

“Good idea,” said Jack. He pulled the research book out of his bag. By the light of the crackling fire, he looked up Basho in the index. “He is here!” Jack turned to the right page and read aloud. “好主意，”杰克说。 他从包里拿出那本研究书。 在噼啪作响的火光下，他在目录里查到了巴肖。 “他在那儿！”杰克翻到正确的那一页，大声读起来。

Basho is one of Japan’s greatest poets.

He wrote short, beautiful poems that

speak to people as clearly today as they

did during the Edo period of Japan. 芭蕉是日本最伟大的诗人之一。他写了许多短小而优美的诗，这些诗今天读起来和江户时代一样清楚动人。

“Basho’ s a great poet! ” said Annie. “That explains everything!” “这解释了一切！”

“Sort of …,” said Jack. “It explains why we had to recite poems to the samurai. But it doesn’t explain why Basho lives in such a dinky house.” “差不多吧……，”杰克说。 “这解释了为什么我们必须给武士背诵诗歌。 但它解释不了为什么巴肖住在这么小的一间房子里。”

Basho opened the door and came in with his bucket. Jack closed the book and slipped it back into his bag. 巴肖打开门，拿着他的桶走了进来。 杰克合上书，把它放回自己的包里。

Basho poured river water into an iron pot over the fire. He pulled three tiny bowls and a small cloth bag from the bamboo chest. He took loose green tea from the bag and dropped it into the bowls. Then he waited patiently for the water to boil. 巴肖把河水倒进火上的铁锅里。 他从竹箱里拿出三个小碗和一个小小的布袋。 他从袋子里拿出一些散装的绿茶，放进碗里。 然后他耐心地等着水烧开。

Jack and Annie waited patiently, too. Listening to the soft rushing sounds of the river outside, Jack started to feel peaceful for the first time all day. 杰克和安妮也耐心地等着。 听着外面小河轻轻流淌的声音，杰克一整天第一次感觉平静下来了。

When the water was hot, Basho poured some into each of the tea bowls. Then he handed the warm bowls to Annie and Jack. 当水烧开后，巴肖把热水倒进每个茶碗里。 然后他把温热的茶碗递给安妮和杰克。

“Thank you,” said Annie. “谢谢，”安妮说。

“Thank you,” said Jack. “谢谢，”杰克说。

“You are welcome,” said Basho. “不用谢，”巴肖说。

Jack carefully took a sip from the steaming bowl. The green tea tasted bitter, but he didn’t mind it. 杰克小心地从冒着热气的茶碗里喝了一口。 绿茶有点苦，但他并不在意。

“Hmm, interesting taste,” said Annie. “Basho, Jack was wondering, if you’re a famous poet, why do you live in such a dinky house?” “嗯，味道真特别，”安妮说。 “巴肖，杰克想知道，如果你是个著名的诗人，为什么住在这么小的房子里呢？”

“Annie!” said Jack, embarrassed. “She’s kidding. I wasn’t really wondering that.” “安妮！”杰克不好意思地说。 “她在开玩笑。 我其实并没有在想那个。”

Basho laughed. “Long ago, I trained to be a samurai,” he said. “But I was not happy. All I wanted to do was write poetry. A poet does not need to live in a castle. A poet needs to live with the wind and the clouds, the flowers and the birds. Here, I have a small garden and my banana tree. I have the sound of the river all day long. Here, I have everything I need to write my poems.” 巴肖笑了。 “很久以前，我接受过武士的训练，”他说。 “但我并不快乐。 我唯一想做的事情就是写诗。 诗人不需要住在城堡里。 诗人需要和风、云、花和鸟一起生活。 这里，我有一个小花园和我的香蕉树。 我整天都能听到河水的声音。 这里，我有写诗所需的一切。

“What do you write about?” asked Annie. “你写什么呢？”安妮问。

“Small things,” said Basho. “A crow picking snails out of the mud, a woodpecker hammering a tree, pine needles scattered by the wind. A poet finds beauty in all the small things of nature.” “小东西，”巴修说。 “一只乌鸦从泥里捡出蜗牛，啄木鸟敲打树木，松针被风吹散。 诗人能在大自然的所有小事物中发现美。

“And you teach poetry to the samurai?” asked Jack. “你教武士写诗吗？”杰克问。

“Yes, the samurai greatly honor the art of poetry,” said Basho. “Poetry helps focus the mind. The samurai believe a truly brave warrior should be able to compose a poem even in the midst of an earthquake, or while facing an enemy on the battlefield.” “是的，武士非常尊重诗歌的艺术，”巴修说。 “诗歌有助于集中注意力。 武士们认为，一个真正勇敢的战士，即使在地震中，或在战场上面对敌人时，也应该能写出一首诗。

“Can you say one of your poems for us?” asked Annie. “你能给我们念一首你的诗吗？”安妮问。

“Let me think,” said Basho. “Well … I was working on a new poem yesterday.” He reached for a wooden box under the table. He took a small piece of delicate paper from the box and read aloud: “让我想想，”巴肖说。 “嗯……我昨天正在写一首新诗。”他伸手从桌子下面拿出一个木盒子。 他从盒子里拿出一张精致的小纸片，大声读起来：

An old pond:

a frog jumps in—

the sound of water. 一个古老的池塘，一只青蛙跳了进去——水声响了一下。

Basho looked up at Jack and Annie. 巴肖抬头看着杰克和安妮。

“Hmm,” said Jack. “Nice beginning.” “嗯，”杰克说。 “开头不错。”

“It is not just the beginning,” said Basho. “It is the whole poem. A small moment in time.” “这不只是开头，”巴肖说。 “这是整首诗。 一个小小的时刻。”

“I think it’s great,” said Annie. “I love frogs. Your poem makes me love them even more.” “我觉得很棒，”安妮说。 “我喜欢青蛙。 你的诗让我更喜欢它们了。”

“Would you read it again, please?” Jack said. He felt like he must have missed something. “你能再读一遍吗？”杰克说。 他觉得好像漏掉了什么。

Basho read again: 巴肖又读了一遍：

An old pond:

a frog jumps in—

the sound of water. 一个古老的池塘，一只青蛙跳了进去——水声响了一下。

Jack nodded thoughtfully. “Good,” he said. “It’s really good.” And he meant it. The poem made him feel as if he himself had been right there, by that pond, hearing the frog splash into the water, breaking the silence. 杰克若有所思地点了点头。 “很好，”他说。 “真的很棒。”他确实是这么觉得的。 这首诗让他感觉好像自己就站在那儿，就在那个池塘边，听着青蛙跳进水里，打破了寂静。

“If you like it, you may have it,” said Basho. He handed the paper to Jack. “如果你喜欢，就可以拿走，”巴肖说。 他把纸递给了杰克。

“Thanks!” said Jack. As he put the poem in his bag, a bell rang softly in the distance. “谢谢！”，杰克说。 当他把诗放进包里时，远处传来一声柔和的钟声。

“Ah, the temple bells,” said Basho. He stood up. “It is time to rest. I will take a mat and sleep outside. I enjoy sleeping under the stars. And now, because of the poem you recited today, Annie, I shall think of them as diamonds in the sky.” “啊，是寺庙的钟声，”巴肖说。他站了起来。 “是时候休息了。” 我要拿一条垫子，睡在外面。 我喜欢在星星下睡觉。 而且现在，因为今天你朗诵的那首诗，安妮，我会把它们想成天空中的钻石。

Annie smiled. 安妮笑了。

“You can stay inside and cover yourselves with these mosquito nets,” said Basho. He pulled some nets from the bamboo chest and handed them to Jack and Annie. “But do not worry, in my small house there are only small mosquitoes— not giant ones like those in the Imperial Palace.” “你们可以待在里面，用这些蚊帐把自己盖起来，”巴肖说。 他从竹箱里拿出一些蚊帐，递给杰克和安妮。 “不过别担心，在我小屋里只有小蚊子——不是像皇宫里那种大蚊子。”

Jack and Annie laughed at Basho’s joke. He gave a net to each of them. Then he picked up one of the mats from the floor and pulled it outside, closing the door behind him. 杰克和安妮听了巴肖的笑话，笑了起来。 他给每人一张网。 然后他从地上捡起一条垫子，把它拉到外面，关上门后走了出去。

The fire in the fireplace had died down. The light from the oil lamp had nearly gone out, too. Jack and Annie lay on the straw mats and covered themselves with the mosquito nets. A cricket chirped on the hearth. Jack noticed a patch of light on the floor. He realized it was moonlight coming through the open window. 壁炉里的火已经熄灭了。 油灯的光也几乎熄灭了。 杰克和安妮躺在稻草垫子上，用蚊帐盖住自己。 壁炉上有一只蟋蟀在叫。 杰克注意到地板上有一块光斑。 他意识到那是月光从敞开的窗户透进来的。

Jack reached out from under the net and put his hand on the square of pale moonlight. He could hear the rustling of the banana plant in the breeze. Half asleep, he imagined himself swaying with its long, broad leaves. 杰克从网子下面伸出一只手，放在那块苍白的月光上。 他能听到微风吹过香蕉植物的沙沙声。 半睡半醒之间，他想象自己正随着那长长的、宽宽的叶子摇摆。

“This dinky hut is much nicer than a castle,” Annie murmured. “I feel like we’re tiny crickets going to sleep.” “这个小屋比城堡舒服多了，”安妮小声说。 “我觉得我们就像两只小蟋蟀，要睡觉了。”

“Yeah … I feel like I’m holding moonlight in my hand,” said Jack, “and like I’m a banana leaf… dancing in the wind.” “是啊……我觉得我好像正捧着月光，”杰克说，“而且好像我是一片香蕉叶……在风中跳舞。”

“Sounds like a poem,” said Annie. “听起来像一首诗，”安妮说。

“Yeah … maybe I should write it down …,” said Jack. But instead, he fell fast asleep. “是啊……也许我应该把它写下来……，”杰克说。 但是，他很快就睡着了。
